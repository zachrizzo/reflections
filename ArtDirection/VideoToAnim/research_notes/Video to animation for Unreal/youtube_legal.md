# Legal and terms-of-service risk of extracting motion from other people's YouTube videos for a commercially sold game (risk briefing, not legal advice)

Research date: 2026-11-21. Audience: a US-based indie developer who sells worldwide. Not legal advice; every "lawyer" flag below is a place where a licensed IP/entertainment attorney should confirm.

Legend. **VERIFIED** = I read the primary source (URL given) on 2026-11-21. Some primary pages were read through a summarising fetch tool, so exact wording of contract text should be copied from the live page before it is relied on. **UNVERIFIED** = secondary source only (law-firm blog, press, search snippet), recalled from memory, or a primary page I could not open. Quotation is deliberately minimal for copyrighted sources (YouTube, Valve, stock sites); statutes, the Copyright Office Compendium and court opinions are public-domain government text and are quoted where load-bearing.

Bottom line (calibrated). (1) For the shipped game, the copyright risk from "pose data extracted from generic motion, applied to a fictional character" is low and mostly theoretical, because motion/poses are largely unprotectable ideas/facts and no footage, face or voice ships. The exception is footage of a choreographed routine or a recognisable person's signature performance. (2) The real exposure of option (f) is process risk, not product risk: breach of YouTube's Terms (contract/enforcement against the developer's accounts/IPs), and a DMCA section 1201 anti-circumvention theory that courts in 2022-2026 have let proceed at the pleading stage, with statutory damages of $200-$2,500 per act of circumvention. That second one is not copyright infringement and, per plaintiffs, is not cured by fair use. It scales with the number of downloads. (3) Likeness law bites only if the output is recognisably a specific person. (4) Cleaner source options exist (own-filmed, hired performer, properly licensed footage) and are cheap relative to the exposure.

## 1. YouTube Terms of Service, downloading tools, enforcement and the yt-dlp legal status

### Takeaway
YouTube's Terms bar downloading/reproducing Content, bar automated access (robots, botnets, scrapers), bar circumventing features that restrict copying, and limit viewing to personal non-commercial use; they contain no exception for "analyse and delete". The Terms do not mention machine learning, but YouTube has said repeatedly that unauthorised downloading/scraping violates them. "yt-dlp works" is a technical fact; whether using it is permitted is a separate question, and US courts are currently letting section 1201 claims over YouTube's "rolling cipher" proceed.

**Statute: DMCA section 1201**
- **VERIFIED** 17 U.S.C. 1201(a)(1)(A): no person shall circumvent a technological measure that effectively controls access to a protected work. "Circumvent" means to avoid, bypass, remove, deactivate or impair the measure "without the authority of the copyright owner" (1201(a)(3)(A)). 1201(c)(1) says the section does not alter infringement defenses including fair use. — [Cornell LII, 17 USC 1201](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/1201)
- **VERIFIED** 17 U.S.C. 1203: "any person injured" by a 1201 violation may sue; statutory damages "not less than $200 or more than $2,500 per act of circumvention" (1203(c)(3)(A)). — [Cornell LII, 17 USC 1203](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/1203)

### Inferences
- The Terms language has no "temporary/analysis-only" carve-out: the prohibition is on downloading/reproducing/accessing, regardless of whether the file is kept. "Analysed and discarded" helps on copyright (see Q3) but not on the contract or on 1201 (circumvention is complete at the moment of access).
- A pipeline that ingests frames by any automated means (including screen-capture of a stream) still arguably "reproduces" Content and uses "automated means"; I found no authority that streaming-based ingestion cures the Terms problem. Whether non-downloading capture avoids 1201 is unresolved (the Cordova defence raised screen recording; the reported ruling did not decide it). Lawyer item.
- Practical enforcement against a small studio is most likely account/IP-level (bans, rate limits), not suits. The 1201 per-act damages are the tail risk: 1,000 downloads at the statutory minimum is $200,000 on paper, so a bulk automated "scrape many videos" pipeline is a different risk profile from a handful of reference clips. Whether anyone would sue a small game studio over discarded files is speculative; the realistic plaintiffs are creators in class actions against large AI firms or a creator who spots misuse.
- If the downloaded video is a CC BY video, the copyright owner has licensed copying; since 1201 liability requires circumvention "without the authority of the copyright owner", there is an argument that the owner's licence supplies authority. I found no case law on this; flag for counsel. It would not fix the Terms (permission is phrased as YouTube plus the rights holder).
- Face/identity clause: if the pipeline runs face detection on people in videos it brushes the "collect or harvest information that might identify a person" clause; a body-keypoints-only pipeline is further from it.

## 2. YouTube's Creative Commons (CC BY) option

### Takeaway
CC BY on YouTube is an uploader-applied licence covering only what the uploader has the right to license; it permits reuse and adaptation, including commercially, with attribution. It does not clear third-party material inside the video, does not override YouTube's own access restrictions, and the label is only as reliable as the uploader.

### Cited Findings
- **VERIFIED** YouTube help: CC BY is an option at upload instead of the default Standard YouTube licence; other creators may reuse the video subject to the CC BY terms. The licence applies only to content the uploader has the right to license (own work, or material that is itself CC BY or public domain); YouTube cannot grant rights to content someone else owns; a video with a Content ID claim cannot be given CC BY; suggested attribution items: title, author, source URL, licence. — [YouTube Help: License types / Creative Commons](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2797468)
- **VERIFIED** CC BY 3.0 Unported legal code: the grant covers reproducing the Work, creating and reproducing Adaptations ("a work based upon the Work"), and distributing/publicly performing; no restriction to non-commercial use. Attribution (4(b)): keep copyright notices and give author name, title, URI where practicable, and for an Adaptation a credit identifying the use of the Work; if credits for all contributing authors appear, the credit must appear as part of those credits "at least as prominent" as theirs; any reasonable manner for the medium. 4(c): no distorting/mutilating action prejudicial to the author's honour or reputation. Section 5: the licensor offers the work as-is with no warranties. — [CC BY 3.0 legal code](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/3.0/legalcode)
- **UNVERIFIED** Which CC version YouTube's "Creative Commons - Attribution" applies today: I recall CC BY 3.0 and a TorrentFreak story about a CC BY 3.0 Blender film supports that for that case, but I found no YouTube page stating the version. — [TorrentFreak on a CC BY 3.0 Blender film re-upload](https://torrentfreak.com/company-hijacks-blenders-cc-by-licensed-film-youtube-strikes-user-221205/)
- **UNVERIFIED (secondary)** Reliability: the label is set by the uploader; mislabelled/re-uploaded videos occur (Blender's CC BY film was re-uploaded by a company that then triggered a strike on a legitimate licensee); YouTube help says it cannot mediate ownership disputes. — [TorrentFreak](https://torrentfreak.com/company-hijacks-blenders-cc-by-licensed-film-youtube-strikes-user-221205/); [YouTube Help: Common copyright myths](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2797449)

### Inferences
- Does CC BY permit extracting motion data and making a derivative? On copyright, yes in principle: pose/motion data derived from a CC BY video, applied to a new character, is at most an "Adaptation" and CC BY allows commercial adaptations with credit. The credit obligation arguably attaches to an Adaptation of the Work; whether abstract skeleton data plus a retargeted animation counts as an Adaptation at all is unsettled, but the conservative course is to credit.
- Credits in a shipped game: add a credits-screen/store-page line per source (title, author, URL, "CC BY 3.0", plus a note that the motion was derived/adapted), prominence comparable to other contributors if a credits list exists. Lawyer to confirm the format and version.
- CC BY does not fix (i) YouTube's Terms on downloading (permission wording is YouTube plus rights holders), (ii) embedded third-party content (music, clips, game footage) not owned by the uploader, (iii) likeness/privacy of people in the video (a CC licence is copyright only), (iv) a mislabelled upload. Mitigation: trace to the creator's own site/original upload, screenshot the licence label and date, keep a provenance log.

### Gaps
- No YouTube page stating the exact CC version or any official YouTube statement on whether CC BY videos may be downloaded outside the platform's own features.
- No case law found on whether a copyright owner's CC licence supplies "authority" for 1201 purposes or whether motion extraction is an "Adaptation".

## 3. Copyright in footage versus copyright in movement

### Takeaway
The copyright in a video is in its audiovisual expression (framing, lighting, sound, editing), which skeleton data does not reproduce. Movements and poses are largely unprotectable (ideas, functional/ordinary motion, social dances, short or simple routines, athletic manoeuvres), but a sufficiently complex, fixed, human-authored choreography is protectable, and a game animation that copies it can infringe (Hanagami). Generic motion carries very low risk; copied choreography or a signature performance carries real risk.

### Cited Findings

**Copyright Office Compendium (Third), Chapter 800 (the PDF I read is dated 01/28/2021; check for later edits)**
- **VERIFIED** 805.1: choreography is the composition and arrangement of a related series of dance movements and patterns organised into a coherent whole; the legislative history says "choreographic works" do not include social dance steps and simple routines. — [Compendium ch. 800 PDF](https://www.copyright.gov/comp3/chap800/ch800-performing-arts.pdf)
- **VERIFIED** 805.5(A): individual movements/steps are not copyrightable (waltz step, hustle, grapevine); the Office cannot register short routines of a few movements "even if the routine is novel or distinctive"; examples refused: the "U-S-A" arm gesture and a football player's touchdown celebration dance. — same PDF
- **VERIFIED** 805.5(B): social dances (including line and break dances), simple routines and other uncopyrightable movements cannot be registered even if they contain substantial creative expression; derivative social dances/simple routines are also unregistrable. The line between choreography and uncopyrightable dance is "a continuum" and many works fall in between. — same PDF
- **VERIFIED** 805.5(B)(3): ordinary motor activities, functional physical movements, competitive maneuvers and feats of skill are not registrable; examples given: exercise routines, aerobic dances, yoga positions (citing Bikram's Yoga v. Evolation, 9th Cir. 2015), football plays, slam dunking, skateboarding or snowboarding (citing NBA v. Motorola, 2d Cir. 1997). Choreography and pantomime are the only works consisting exclusively of bodily movement that qualify. — same PDF
- **VERIFIED** 805.4(C): the work must be created by a human for human performers. 805.4(D): choreography that incorporates uncopyrightable steps can be protected as a whole if it has enough choreographic authorship. 805.3: a choreographic work must be fixed (audiovisual recordings qualify as one form of fixation). — same PDF

**Hanagami v. Epic Games (9th Cir. Nov 1, 2023, No. 22-55890)**
- **VERIFIED** The court reversed a motion-to-dismiss ruling in a suit over a Fortnite "emote" (an in-game animation). It adopted the Compendium's definitions of "choreography" and "dance", held choreography is protected through the selection and arrangement of otherwise unprotectable elements, rejected reducing it to static "poses" (analogy: reducing music to notes), listed elements such as body position, shape, actions, transitions, use of space, timing, pauses, energy, motif and repetition, held the short length of the copied portion did not by itself defeat protection ("short does not always equate to simple"), and declined to decide whether protection is thin or broad. — [Ninth Circuit opinion PDF](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2023/11/01/22-55890.pdf)
- **UNVERIFIED (secondary)** The case settled in February 2024 with the parties telling the court it could not be refiled, so there is no merits ruling on infringement; I found no 2025-26 developments. — [Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/ip-law/epic-games-resolves-choreographer-suit-over-fortnite-dance-move); [Justia News](https://news.justia.com/choreographer-settles-copyright-lawsuit-concerning-fortnite-dance-emote)

**Fortnite/NBA 2K dance suits**
- **UNVERIFIED (secondary)** Ribeiro v. Epic (the "Carlton"): the Copyright Office refused registration in Feb 2019 (about three simple steps); Ribeiro voluntarily dismissed in March 2019. 2 Milly (Terrence Ferguson) and Russell Horning ("Backpack Kid") also dropped their suits against Epic; I could not open dockets, so with/without prejudice is unknown. A parallel suit against Take-Two over NBA 2K was filed; I found no outcome. **Tattoo cases (nearest game-industry analogy for "a real person's creative detail copied into a game")**
- **VERIFIED** Alexander v. Take-Two (S.D. Ill., No. 18-cv-966, Sept 25, 2024): a jury found for the tattoo artist over tattoos reproduced on Randy Orton in WWE 2K; the court denied judgment as a matter of law and fair use was not found (factors 1-3 weighed against fair use; factor 4 favoured defendants as no licensing market existed). The tattoos were also usable in the Create-a-Superstar tool, which pushed the use beyond depicting Orton. Damages figure not read (a blog headline says no or minimal damages: UNVERIFIED). — [Copyright Office Fair Use Index summary](https://www.copyright.gov/fair-use/summaries/Alexander-v-Take-Two-Interactive-Software-Inc-No-18-CV-966-S-D-Ill-Sept-25-2024.pdf)
- **UNVERIFIED (secondary)** Solid Oak Sketches v. 2K (S.D.N.Y.): Take-Two won on de minimis use and implied licence via the NBA's licensing of players' likenesses. — [Copyright Office Fair Use Index summary](https://www.copyright.gov/fair-use/summaries/solid-oak-sketches-2k-games-sdny2020.pdf); [Loeb](https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/publications/2020/10/alexander-v-taketwo-interactive-software)

**Fair use and the downloaded copy**
- **UNVERIFIED (secondary)** Bartz v. Anthropic (N.D. Cal., June 2025): training on lawfully acquired books was held strongly transformative fair use, partly because no surplus copy was kept and the source copy was destroyed, while retaining a permanent library of pirated books was not fair use; Kadrey v. Meta found fair use on a thin record, suggesting a different record could change the result. District-court rulings, not binding precedent, and about LLM training, not pose extraction. — [Debevoise](https://www.debevoise.com/insights/publications/2025/06/anthropic-and-meta-decisions-on-fair-use); [Fenwick](https://www.fenwick.com/insights/publications/two-federal-courts-rule-that-reproduction-of-books-to-train-llms-is-fair-use-but-with-caveats-and-strikingly-different-views)
- **UNVERIFIED (statute, from memory)** 17 USC 102(b): copyright does not extend to any idea, procedure, process, system or method of operation; the merger and scenes-a-faire doctrines and Feist (facts are not original) limit protection of poses and stock movement. I did not fetch these in this session.

### Inferences
- Is extracted motion data a copy of the footage? Skeleton keypoints/joint rotations are numbers describing positions over time; they do not reproduce pixels, sound, composition or editing, so they are not a copy of the audiovisual work's expression. The downloaded file itself is a reproduction of the video (a copy is made), but a transient, non-distributed, destroyed analysis copy has a decent fair-use argument (Bartz-style reasoning, with the caveats above). Whether that analysis copy is infringing is a separate and smaller question than the contract/1201 problems.
- Is the motion a derivative work of the footage? Weak for generic motion; stronger if the footage records a protected choreographic work, because then the animation is a copy of the choreography (a work distinct from the video). Hanagami is the on-point warning: an in-game animation that tracks a registered choreography closely can be substantially similar.
- Category map. (1) Generic motion (walk, run, sit, idle, punch, fight stance, ordinary gestures): unprotectable under Compendium 805.5(B)(3) and 102(b); risk very low. (2) Social/line/break dances, simple routines, sports manoeuvres, exercise/yoga: unregistrable per 805.5; low. (3) Choreographed or complex performances (a dance routine from a music video, a pantomime/skit, a performed script): protectable if fixed, human-authored and sufficiently complex; this is where Hanagami-type claims arise; high if copied closely, especially a short viral routine paired with the song. (4) Martial-arts kata/forms: not addressed in what I read; Bikram suggests a fixed sequence presented as a system/process may be unprotectable, but a kata choreographed as a performance piece may differ; unsettled, lawyer item. (5) Signature moves of a famous athlete/streamer: protection as choreography is weak (Compendium refuses celebratory dances and competitive manoeuvres), but identity-based claims (Q4) can apply.
- Averaging, blending, retargeting and cleaning will not reliably launder (3): the test looks at selection and arrangement; changing skeleton and rig does not change the sequence. Using fragments of generic motion from many sources lowers the chance of copying any single protectable sequence.
- Pose estimation is not itself the exposure in the game; the exposure is which clips go in.

### Gaps
- No court has addressed motion data extracted from video, retargeted mocap, or pose-estimation output specifically (I found none): this is unsettled.
- 2 Milly/Horning docket details and any 2025-26 Fortnite/NBA 2K emote rulings not found; Hanagami settlement terms unknown.
- Did not verify 17 USC 102(b), Feist or scenes-a-faire primary text.

## 4. Likeness, personality rights and digital replicas

### Takeaway
Right of publicity and the new digital-replica laws protect identifiable people (name, voice, image, likeness; some states add more). Generic, unrecognisable body motion on a fictional character is very unlikely to be a "digital replica" under statutes that require a highly realistic, readily identifiable depiction. Risk rises where a recognisable gait, signature move or look is paired with a lookalike character or a name association. Federal NO FAKES is not law as of the latest coverage I found (committee-approved June 2026).

### Inferences
- Is retargeted body motion on an unrecognisable generic character a "digital replica"? Under the California-style definition (highly realistic, readily identifiable as a particular person's voice or visual likeness), a stylised or fictional character whose motion is not recognisable as a specific individual is very unlikely to qualify. The SAG-AFTRA IMA excludes traditional animation/rigging, and in any case binds only signatories (not a non-signatory indie, though a union performer you hire might be covered by other agreements). Likeness statutes also generally speak to the person's voice/visual likeness, not their bare kinematics; whether a distinctive gait alone can be a "likeness" is untested in anything I found.
- Escalation factors, roughly in order: (1) source is a celebrity, athlete, streamer or any named person, and you keep their identity in your records or marketing; (2) the clip is a signature move or recognisable routine; (3) the in-game character resembles them (look, costume, name, voice); (4) real-time or on-demand generation of that person's motion; (5) the person is deceased and in CA/NY/TN. A game that already uses real-person lookalikes (Keller line) is where claims have succeeded.
- For hired performers, AB 2602-style contracts and SAG-AFTRA norms say get a specific written description of how the motion will be used, including that it will be retargeted to other characters and possibly processed by AI/ML.
- Privacy overlay (UNVERIFIED, not researched): if you retain face or body-geometry data of identifiable people, state biometric laws (e.g., Illinois BIPA) and GDPR may apply; discarding the video and keeping only anonymous joint rotations reduces this.

## 5. Practical risk ladder (lowest to highest)

### Takeaway
Order by exposure: (a) own-filmed, (b) hired performer with a written agreement, (c) CC0/public-domain footage from a reliable archive, (e) paid licensed stock footage you have read the licence for, (d) CC BY YouTube footage with attribution, (f) arbitrary copyrighted YouTube footage analysed and discarded. The jump to (f) is mainly process risk (Terms, 1201), not shipped-product copyright risk. (c) and (e) are close; their order depends on the specific licence and on provenance certainty.

### Cited Findings
- Stock licences read (primary pages): **VERIFIED** Pexels licence allows modifying photos and videos, attribution not required; forbids selling unaltered copies, redistributing on other stock platforms, implying endorsement, and showing identifiable people in a bad or offensive light; the page says nothing about machine learning/data extraction or about model releases. — [Pexels licence](https://www.pexels.com/license/)
- **VERIFIED** Pixabay Content Licence summary: may modify or adapt content into new works, no attribution; may not sell or distribute standalone; restricted for recognisable trademarks; do not use recognisable people in immoral or illegal ways; you must check whether you need third-party consent or licences; the summary does not address AI/ML or model releases (full Terms not read). — [Pixabay licence summary](https://pixabay.com/service/license-summary/)
- **UNVERIFIED (secondary)** Shutterstock's standard licence reportedly prohibits using content as training data for AI/ML (third-party summary; official licence page returned 403); Shutterstock separately sells AI-training data licences. — [LicenseOrg summary](https://licenseorg.com/guide/images/shutterstock); [Shutterstock data licensing](https://www.shutterstock.com/ko/data-licensing/image-generation)
- **UNVERIFIED (secondary)** Pond5: its "Video AI Terms of Service" (a separate product) bar using content or outputs to create or train ML systems; I could not read the standard content licence (403) so whether it has a data-mining/AI clause is unknown. — [Pond5 Video AI Terms](https://www.pond5.com/legal/video-ai-terms-of-service)
- **UNVERIFIED (secondary)** Commercial video-to-mocap services have their own terms (Move AI's API policy reportedly bars using the API to train mocap/pose-estimation AI; Plask's help page says animations belong to users but its Terms reportedly limit use to personal non-commercial; these conflict). I did not confirm whether any of them requires the uploader to own the video rights. — [Move AI API acceptable use](https://developers.move.ai/policies/api-acceptable-use); [Plask FAQ](https://knowledge.plask.ai/can-i-use-my-plask-animations-for-commercial-use); [Plask Terms](https://plask.ai/docs/100002)
- **UNVERIFIED (conflicting secondary)** Ready-made mocap datasets: sources conflict on whether the CMU Graphics Lab database is free for all uses including commercial or research-only, and the FBX conversion is listed as CC BY-NC; AMASS has its own licence page (not read; I believe non-commercial). Verify at source before use. — [CMU mocap resources](https://mocap.cs.cmu.edu/resources.php); [AMASS licence](https://amass.is.tue.mpg.de/license.html); [FBX conversion (4TU)](https://data.4tu.nl/datasets/0448aab2-3332-449f-a8e2-d208cb58c7df)

### Inferences
Ranking with main exposure and what a lawyer should confirm:
1. (a) Own-filmed reference. Exposure: essentially only third parties who appear (release), your own privacy if you care, and choreography if you perform someone else's routine. Confirm: any person appearing signs a release; do not perform a third party's registered choreography.
2. (b) Hired performer with a written mocap/release agreement. Exposure: ownership (work-for-hire/assignment, performer is a contractor), scope-of-use clause (retargeting, AI/ML processing, future games), CA AB 2602-style specificity, union status (SAG-AFTRA signatory or not), moral rights outside the US, choreography ownership if they bring routines. Confirm: assignment language, consent to digital processing, no reuse restrictions, tax/contractor status.
3. (c) CC0/public-domain footage. Exposure: provenance (is it truly CC0/PD, by the actual rights holder?), identifiable people and music in the clip (CC0 does not clear likeness/privacy), host-site terms for access. YouTube has no CC0 option (its help describes Standard vs CC BY), so CC0 footage would come from archives. Confirm: provenance and releases.
4. (e) Licensed stock footage. Exposure: licence scope (derivative works are usually allowed; some licences, e.g. Shutterstock reportedly, bar AI/ML training or data use; "data extraction" wording varies), whether a model release exists for people shown (Pexels/Pixabay disclaim), whether the licence permits use inside a software product sold separately. Confirm: read the exact licence, buy licences that include releases and an explicit "derivative works/software products" right, and get written confirmation (support email) that extracting motion data is allowed.
5. (d) CC BY footage with attribution. Exposure: uploader may not own all of it (embedded music/clips, Content ID), mislabelled re-uploads, attribution duty in the shipped game, version of CC licence, and the access route: if you pull it from YouTube with an automation tool you inherit the Terms/1201 questions of (f) unless the creator authorised access. Confirm: attribution format, tracing to the original creator, whether permission can be had from the creator in writing (cheapest fix).
6. (f) Arbitrary copyrighted YouTube footage, analysed and discarded. Exposure: (i) Terms breach and enforcement against accounts/IPs (real, low cost); (ii) 1201 circumvention claim, real but historically targeted at large scrapers or at downloaders who reused footage; pleading-stage rulings in Cordova (Jan 2026) and Yout (2022) exist; statutory $200-$2,500 per act; plaintiff may be the video's creator and says fair use is no defence; scales with bulk downloads; (iii) copyright in the transient download (fair-use argument decent, unsettled); (iv) choreography/signature copying if the clip is a routine (Hanagami); (v) likeness if a recognisable person; (vi) a platform rights warranty (below). Mostly theoretical: (iii) and generic-motion copyright. Real but modest for a handful of clips: (i). Meaningful and scales with volume: (ii). Confirm: whether any automated download tool is used (1201), volume, whether the clip set avoids routines and named people, and whether a documented clean-room alternative is cheaper.
- Cross-cutting calibration. Contract/enforcement risk (Terms) targets the developer's accounts and tooling; it does not by itself make the shipped animation infringing. The shipped game's copyright exposure is driven by what is copied (choreography, identity), not by how the video was obtained. A claim over process could still be commercially painful (discovery, injunction demands, takedown friction) even if unlikely to succeed on the merits; that is why a provenance log and a cleaner source for any key animation matters.
- Keep a provenance sheet per clip (URL, date, uploader, licence label screenshot, what motion was used for which animation) and discard media on a schedule; this supports fair use and CC attribution and helps answer a platform's questions.

### Gaps
- No empirical data on how often individual developers are pursued for 1201 or Terms breach over analysis-only downloads; I found none.
- Standard licence text for Shutterstock and Pond5 was not accessible; Pixabay full Terms and Pexels Terms of Service (beyond the licence page) not read.
- No confirmation of what commercial video-to-mocap vendors require of uploaded footage.

## 6. Platform angles: Steam, itch.io, Epic AI MegaJam; EU/UK notes

### Takeaway
Steam only asks about AI use that produces content players consume, and expressly de-emphasises developer efficiency tools; a video-derived animation shipped in the game is arguably "pre-generated AI-assisted content" and may need an honest disclosure answer. Both stores have the developer warrant it has the rights to what it ships; neither is likely to audit motion provenance, but a warranty plus indemnity makes provenance matter if a claim arrives.

### Cited Findings
- **UNVERIFIED (article quoting Valve; Valve's own page rendered empty for me)** In January 2026 Valve rewrote the Steamworks AI disclosure form: it focuses on AI used to create content that ships with the game and is consumed by players (artwork, sound, narrative, localisation), with separate sections for pre-generated content and live-generated content, and states that efficiency gains from using such tools are not the focus. The article was published Jan 17, 2026 and found no Valve wording on IP rights; PC Gamer reports pre-generated content is held to the same rules as non-AI content, live-generated content needs safeguards against illegal output. — [GamingOnLinux, Jan 17 2026](https://www.gamingonlinux.com/2026/01/valve-tweak-steam-ai-disclosure-form-for-developers-to-clarify-its-for-content-consumed-by-players); [PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/software/ai/steam-updates-ai-disclosure-form-to-specify-that-its-focused-on-ai-generated-content-that-is-consumed-by-players-not-efficiency-tools-used-behind-the-scenes/)

**EU / UK differences (brief)**
- **UNVERIFIED (secondary)** EU: the DSM Directive Article 4 allows text-and-data-mining copies by anyone with lawful access unless rights are reserved (machine-readable for online content). In Kneschke v. LAION (Hamburg, 2024) the court held a research exception applied, and also treated a website's natural-language terms reservation as capable of being machine-readable; later cases are split. Whether pose extraction counts as "text and data mining", and whether YouTube's Terms operate as a reservation, are open questions; EU anti-circumvention rules (InfoSoc Directive Article 6, from memory) and the 2020 Hamburg youtube-dl decision point the same way as 1201. — [Wolters Kluwer Copyright Blog on LAION](https://legalblogs.wolterskluwer.com/copyright-blog/kneschke-vs-laion-landmark-ruling-on-tdm-exceptions-for-ai-training-data-part-2/); [EU IP Helpdesk](https://intellectual-property-helpdesk.ec.europa.eu/news-events/news/data-mining-and-eu-copyright-legal-framework-laion-decision-2024-10-15_en)
- **VERIFIED** UK: section 29A CDPA 1988 permits a lawfully accessed copy for computational analysis only "for the sole purpose of research for a non-commercial purpose" with acknowledgement, and makes contract terms that purport to restrict that permitted copying unenforceable to that extent; it does not cover commercial development. The UK has no general US-style fair use. I did not check any 2025-26 UK TDM reform. — [legislation.gov.uk s.29A](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1988/48/section/29A)
- **UNVERIFIED (not researched)** Neither the EU nor the UK has a single federal-style right of publicity (image/personality protection is national, plus GDPR for identifiable people and passing-off in the UK). Selling worldwide means the strictest of these can apply to a recognisable-person case; for generic motion this adds little.

### Inferences
- Steam AI disclosure: pose estimation is an ML model producing in-game animation, so a cautious reading is to disclose pre-generated AI-assisted content for any shipped animation made that way, in plain terms; the efficiency-tool carve-out is better suited to coding/dev workflow tools. Confirm on the live Steamworks Content Survey page at submission. Disclosure is about AI use, not about whether the source footage was licensed; the rights question sits in Valve's agreement.
- Rights warranties are generic "you own or have rights" statements plus indemnity; the likely trigger for scrutiny is a third-party complaint, not an audit. A provenance log answers it quickly.
- EU/UK: the US analysis (Terms, 1201, choreography, likeness) carries over; the main practical difference is that EU/UK lack broad fair use, and UK commercial TDM has no exception, so for a developer selling in those markets "analysed and discarded" has a weaker copyright footing than in the US.
